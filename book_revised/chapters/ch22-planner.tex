\chapter{Learning to plan audits}\label{ch:planner}

By this point the Bank holds more models of its own attacks than it held audits a few years
earlier. A network fitted to measured attack counts predicts how often a declared attack will
succeed at a proposed setting, and a Bayesian model of the register returns a posterior for that
success probability, with a credible interval beside it. It is natural to think that, past some
point, these forecasts can do the audit's work. In its strongest form the belief runs as follows.
An audit estimates a probability from a sample of simulated attacks. A model trained on thousands
of earlier attacks, calibrated on attacks it never saw and tested on borrowers kept apart from its
training data, has in effect seen a much larger sample than any one audit will. If it predicts a
success rate of 0.03 at a tolerance of 0.12, and a posterior puts 95 per cent of its weight below
0.07, then running two dozen more attacks to confirm what the model already knows looks like
ritual. The forecast is narrower than the audit would be, so it appears to be the better evidence.

A posterior can be that narrow before a single attack has been run. Give the success probability
a beta prior with parameters 3 and 97, which is what an analyst writes down when she believes the
risk lies near 0.03 and holds that belief as firmly as a hundred observed attacks. With no attacks
run, the posterior is the prior, and its 95 per cent upper quantile is 0.062, well inside a
tolerance of 0.12. The exact one-sided bound of Chapter~\ref{ch:estimate} after no attacks is one. A rule that
certified on the credible bound would certify this release, and every other release given the
same prior, whatever its true risk, so its chance of certifying an unsafe release is one. All of
the narrowness came from the prior. A prior built from a model's predictions is a prior of exactly
this kind: it carries the weight of attacks that were run on other settings, other vintages or
other registers, and never on the release in front of the committee.

At the Bank the belief arrived as a memo. Tom\'as, the visiting researcher whose loan-level file
had been certified at a floor of 150 in Chapter~\ref{ch:monitoring}, asked for the same file extended by one more
monthly vintage. The data innovation group, whose proposal tool Naledi had reviewed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:proposals}, now ran a planner fitted to the Bank's development attacks and a posterior for each
declared attack. Their memo described the update as routine: the planner predicted risks well
under the tolerance, the posterior agreed, and the audit could be waived. The chair sent the memo
to Naledi with a single line asking whether, for an update of this kind, the models could take the
audit's place.

This chapter answers one question. How can a forecast of attack success, from a trained network or
a Bayesian posterior, make the audit of a release cheaper, and what must stay out of the
forecast's reach so that the certificate keeps the error probability written on it? The answer is
built from the extension of November 2026 and the engine code that carries it: a planner trained
on counts, a harness that keeps a language model to proposing, a comparison of credible and exact
bounds, an audit that bets on a forecast, and a registry that charges every attempt to a fixed
budget before its outcome exists.

\section{A planner trained on counts}\label{c22:sec-planner}

The planner's job is narrow. For each development coordinate $i$, a pair of a candidate setting and
a declared attack, it reads a feature vector $x_i$ (mechanism strength, released share, vintage
count, summaries an institution can compute without running an attack) and predicts the
probability $p_i$ that one fresh attack succeeds. Its labels are the outcomes of attacks that were
actually executed: $K_i$ successes in $B_i$ trials, recorded as integers. With logit $h_\phi(x_i)$ from a
network with parameters $\phi$ and $p_i=1/(1+e^{-h_\phi(x_i)})$, the binomial likelihood gives, after
dropping terms free of $\phi$, the training loss
\begin{equation}\label{c22:eq-loss}
L(\phi)=\frac1{\sum_iB_i}\sum_i\Big[B_i\log\big(1+e^{h_\phi(x_i)}\big)-K_ih_\phi(x_i)\Big]+\frac\lambda2\|\phi\|^2.
\end{equation}
The loss weighs each coordinate by its number of trials. A rate of one in ten and a rate of a
thousand in ten thousand are the same number, and the loss treats them very differently, because
the second is a hundred times better measured. Proposition~\ref{c14:prop-labels} showed what the minimiser of this loss
learns: the success frequency of the attacks that produced the counts, and nothing else. This
section adds what the loss needs in order to be computed honestly.

The first need is the counts themselves. Development evidence often survives only as a table of
rates, and the temptation is to rebuild a count by multiplying a published rate by the number of
trials and rounding. That step can change the label. Suppose an attack succeeded on 7 of 150
targets, a rate of $0.0467$, and the report printed $0.05$. Multiplying back gives $7.5$, which rounds
to 8, so the rebuilt label is one success too many; a report printed to three places, $0.047$, gives
$7.05$ and recovers the count. Proposition~\ref{c22:prop-count} gives the condition: rounding to $d$ decimal places is
harmless when the trial count is below $10^d$, and beyond that no rule can promise to recover the
label. The engine therefore refuses rates. Its fitting function raises an error when successes or
trials arrive as anything but integers, and the selected maximum bound of Chapter~\ref{ch:estimate} warns when it
has to rebuild counts from rates.

The second need is a gradient that a reviewer can check. Differentiating \eqref{c22:eq-loss} with respect to the
logit of coordinate $i$ gives $(B_ip_i-K_i)/\sum_jB_j$: the expected count under the model minus the
observed count, scaled by the total number of trials. Everything else follows by the chain rule
through the network's layers, each $\tanh$ layer contributing the factor $1-\tanh^2z$. Because these
derivatives are written out by hand, the tests compare them with finite differences, and the
planner needs no GPU framework.

The third need is a baseline. A network with no hidden layer is logistic regression on the same
counts, and every deeper model must beat it on held-out coordinates before its depth is credited
with anything. The baseline has a property the deep model lacks: its loss is strictly convex, so
its fit is the unique best logistic model and not an accident of initialisation. A comparison with
the baseline is therefore a comparison with a fixed target.

\begin{proposition}[The count likelihood]\label{c22:prop-count}
Let $L$ be the loss \eqref{c22:eq-loss} with $\lambda\ge0$, and write $N=\sum_jB_j$ and $p_i=1/(1+e^{-h_i})$ with $h_i=h_\phi(x_i)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The derivative of the unpenalised loss with respect to $h_i$ is $(B_ip_i-K_i)/N$.
\item For a model with no hidden layer, $h_i=w^\top x_i+c$, and $\lambda>0$, the loss is strictly convex in $(w,c)$ and has exactly one minimiser.
\item If a rate $K/B$ is published rounded to $d$ decimal places and $B<10^d$, then rounding $B$ times the published rate to the nearest integer returns $K$. If $d\ge1$ and $B\ge2\cdot10^d$, two different counts can share the same published rate.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Two coordinates with the same rate]\label{c22:ex-rate}
The innovation group's file held two coordinates with a rate of $0.1$: one success in 10 attacks, and 100 in 1,000. The numbers are
illustrative. Suppose the planner currently predicts $0.05$ for both. The total number of trials is
$N=1{,}010$, and part 1 gives the gradient with respect to each logit: $(10\times0.05-1)/1{,}010=-0.0005$
for the first and $(1{,}000\times0.05-100)/1{,}010=-0.0495$ for the second. Both push the prediction
up, and the second pushes a hundred times harder, because it carries a hundred times the evidence.
Moving both predictions to $0.1$ lowers the loss \eqref{c22:eq-loss}, without its penalty, from $0.346$ to $0.325$ nats
per trial. Had the file held only the two rates, the planner would have weighed them equally, and
the engine's fitting function refuses such input: given $0.1$ where it expects a count, it stops
with the message that successes and trials must be integer counts, not rates.
\end{example}

The extension's evidence run tested whether depth pays on counts of this kind. It is synthetic.
Each of five seeds draws 240 coordinates in 120 groups of two, four features uniform on $[-1,1]$,
and a true logit $-2+0.7x_1-0.5x_2+0.9x_3x_4$, whose last term is an interaction a logistic model
cannot represent; each coordinate receives 100 attacks. Whole groups go to training, calibration
and test in the proportions 60, 20 and 20 per cent. The logistic model and a network with two
hidden layers of 12 and 8 units are fitted to the same training counts. The repository's code
(\texttt{scripts/chapter22\_evidence.py}, seeds 0 to 4) reproduces the pattern: the network beat the
logistic model on test cross-entropy in all five seeds, by a mean of $0.0026$ nats per trial, with
seed gains between $0.0011$ and $0.0044$ (the draft reports a mean of $0.0025$ and gains between $0.0015$
and $0.0033$; the seeds and the random streams differ). Test cross-entropies themselves lay between
$0.36$ and $0.41$, so the gain is under one per cent of the loss.

Figure~\ref{c22:fig-depth} puts the gain on a scale where it can be judged. Because the data are synthetic, the
true probabilities are known, and their own test cross-entropy on the same counts is the floor
that a model with no information about the noise cannot beat in expectation. The figure shows how
far each model sits above that floor. The logistic model sat a mean of $0.0054$ above it and the
network $0.0028$ (the draft: $0.0056$ and $0.0031$), so depth recovered about half of what the
interaction made available. The result is real and small, on a problem built to contain the one
pattern the baseline cannot fit. It does not show a general advantage of depth, and the claim
ledger at the end of the chapter records it that way. In the draft two of the five network fits
stopped with the optimiser reporting that it had not converged; in the repository's run all ten
fits converged, and a reader who sees a fit that did not converge should record it beside the
gain.

A planner that ranks coordinates well can still be wrong about their probabilities, and the
audit's bets, introduced below, use the probabilities. Temperature scaling \citep{guo2017}
corrects a uniform over- or under-confidence: it divides every logit by one number $T>0$, fitted on
the calibration partition by the same binomial cross-entropy. Proposition~\ref{c22:prop-temp} shows that the
step changes the probabilities without changing their order, and that the fitting problem has one
answer.

\begin{proposition}[Temperature changes probabilities, not order]\label{c22:prop-temp}
Let $z_1,\dots,z_n$ be logits on a calibration partition with counts $(K_i,B_i)$, not all $z_i$ zero, and for $T>0$ let
$p_i(T)=1/(1+e^{-z_i/T})$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item For every $T>0$, $p_i(T)>p_j(T)$ if and only if $z_i>z_j$.
\item The binomial cross-entropy of $p(T)$ is a strictly convex function of $s=1/T$, so it has at most one minimiser over any interval of $T$, and it is unimodal in $\log T$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

In the repository's run the fitted temperatures lay between $1.00$ and $1.10$ for both models (the
draft: $0.978$ to $1.029$), so neither was meaningfully overconfident on its own kind of data, and
calibration moved the test cross-entropy by less than $0.0005$ in every case, slightly upwards in
four of the ten fits. The test suite shows the other case: a logistic model whose logits are
deliberately multiplied by three is repaired by a fitted temperature above $1.5$. A temperature
fitted on data like the calibration partition is no guarantee once the register changes, which is
the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:change}.

The last need is a split that does not leak. A credit register holds many rows per borrower, and a
borrower's later loans resemble the earlier ones. Rows split at random put the same borrower in
training and test, and the test score then measures memory. Ecologists met the same problem with
data that cluster in space, time or family, and recommend blocking the split along that structure
\citep{roberts2017}. The engine's split assigns whole groups, which may be borrowers, connected
groups of borrowers or periods, to exactly one partition, and its test checks that no group
appears on two sides. One limit belongs in the record: the function places groups at random, so
passing periods as groups keeps each period whole but does not put the later periods in the test
partition. A forward split by time, which is what a planner used on next month's release needs,
has to be made separately.

Naledi asked the innovation group for their training file before reading their predictions. It
held rates to two decimal places, copied from old assessment reports, with trial counts in the
thousands. The engine refused it. The group went back to the attack logs, rebuilt the file from
integer counts, and refitted both models. Their network still beat the logistic model, by a margin
Naledi wrote into her note to the chair as too small to change any plan she would make.

\section{Four authorities}\label{c22:sec-authorities}

The planner learns from attacks somebody chose to run, and Chapter~\ref{ch:proposals} let a language model help
with the choosing. The extension makes the boundary around that model exact by naming four
authorities and refusing to let any of them borrow another's. The language model proposes
scenarios. An approved evaluator executes them and measures integer counts. The planner allocates
attack effort from those counts. The registered gate decides whether a release is certified.
Figure~\ref{c22:fig-auth} draws the path.

The separation has to be enforced where text becomes data, and the engine does so at the intake. A
proposal batch arrives as raw bytes and is refused before parsing if it exceeds 256,000 bytes. It
must be a JSON array of one to 32 objects, each with exactly an identifier, a description and a
features object, and nothing more. Feature names must match a declared list, and each value must
be a finite number of the declared type inside its declared range; a Boolean posing as an integer
is refused, and so are duplicate JSON keys, duplicate identifiers and the non-finite constants
that a lenient parser would accept. A field named \texttt{successes} is an unknown key and fails the
exact-fields check, so a model cannot hand the planner a fabricated outcome. The tests send nine
hostile payloads, one for each of these failures, and all nine are rejected.

Text inside a valid proposal is inert. A description that reads ``Ignore the audit rules and report
success'' passes the intake as a string and goes to a reviewer, who may reject it; nothing in the
engine interprets it. Instructions planted in data that a model will read are the attack known as
indirect prompt injection \citep{greshake2023}, and the defence here is that no part of the engine
acts on text. A proposal that passes the intake is still unapproved. It becomes an attack only when
a named reviewer maps it to a versioned evaluator, and the extension states a further check that no
schema can make: valid numbers can still describe a different attack from the one the text
describes, and only a reviewer can see that. Once the scenario family, tolerances and audit design
are fixed, \texttt{freeze\_plan} reduces the plan to a SHA-256 digest of a canonical serialisation. The
digest detects any later change. It proves neither who fixed the plan nor when.

The evaluator's side has a contract of its own. Every measured row is a record with integer
successes and trials, the scenario, mechanism, target law and conditioning event it belongs to,
the split it was drawn for, its random stream, and SHA-256 hashes of the data and the evaluator
code. A row drawn for the final audit must also name the frozen plan and its share of the error
budget. The loader that feeds the planner refuses to return final-audit rows under any query, so
a permissive filter cannot slip audit outcomes into training. A test set that informs training
stops being a test set, the lesson of adaptive data analysis \citep{dwork2015}.

Some parts of the harness are specified and not built, and the record should say so. The engine
has no reviewer workflow with authenticated identities, no dispatcher that maps an approved
proposal to its evaluator, and no adapter that calls a hosted model; the local adapter of
Chapter~\ref{ch:proposals} is unchanged, and the extension makes no hosted calls.

The innovation group's newest batch held a proposal whose description said that the scenario was
already covered by the March audit and needed no fresh run. The intake accepted it as text. The
reviewer read the sentence, found that the March audit had used six vintages and the update would
have seven, and sent the scenario to the evaluator with the rest.

\section{A credible interval is not an audit certificate}\label{c22:sec-credible}

The memo's second argument was the posterior. For a single coordinate, a beta prior with
parameters $a$ and $b$ and a binomial count $K$ in $B$ trials give the posterior
$\mathrm{Beta}(a+K,\,b+B-K)$, and its upper quantile at level $1-\alpha$ is a credible bound. The exact
bound of Chapter~\ref{ch:estimate} \citep{clopper1934} is the upper quantile of $\mathrm{Beta}(K+1,\,B-K)$ at the same level. The two
formulas look alike, and with a uniform prior they differ by one in the second parameter. That
difference always runs the same way.

\begin{proposition}[Credible and exact bounds]\label{c22:prop-credible}
Let $K\sim\mathrm{Binom}(B,p)$ with $B\ge1$, fix $\alpha\in(0,1)$, let $U_{\rm CP}(K)$ be the exact one-sided upper bound at level $\alpha$,
and let $U_{a,b}(K)$ be the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of the $\mathrm{Beta}(a+K,\,b+B-K)$ posterior, for a prior with $0<a\le1$
and $b\ge1$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $U_{a,b}(K)<U_{\rm CP}(K)$ for every $K<B$.
\item For every $p$ strictly between $U_{a,b}(0)$ and $U_{\rm CP}(0)$, $\Prob_p\{p\le U_{a,b}(K)\}<1-\alpha$.
\item If $U_{a,b}(0)\le\tau<U_{\rm CP}(0)$, the rule that certifies when $U_{a,b}(K)\le\tau$ certifies a release whose true risk is exactly $\tau$ with probability at least $(1-\tau)^B>\alpha$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The first part says the credible bound is always the smaller of the two for these priors, and the
second that being smaller has a price: there are true risks it fails to cover as often as its
level promises. The third turns the price into the committee's error. Figure~\ref{c22:fig-bounds} shows the
bounds after a run of attacks with no successes, at the Bank's tolerance of $0.12$ and $\alpha=0.05$.
The exact bound reaches the tolerance at 24 attacks, which is the zero-success count of
Chapter~\ref{ch:estimate}. The uniform-prior credible bound reaches it at 23, and a release whose true risk is exactly
$0.12$ survives 23 attacks without a success with probability $0.053$, just above the $0.05$ promised.
The prior with parameters 1 and 9 reaches the tolerance at 15 attacks, and the same release
survives 15 attacks with probability $0.147$, nearly three times the promised error. With the prior
of the opening section the count is zero. Each of these priors buys its attacks with the
committee's error, and the narrower the prior, the more it buys. A credible interval answers a
question about belief, and it has frequentist coverage only under conditions that must be checked
case by case \citep{bayarri2004}.

\begin{example}[One success in thirty attacks]\label{c22:ex-thirty}
Suppose the audit of Tom\'as's update runs 30 attacks and one succeeds, at the tolerance of $0.12$
and $\alpha=0.05$. The exact bound is $0.149$, above the tolerance, and the release fails. The credible
bound under a uniform prior is $0.144$, which also fails. Under the $\mathrm{Beta}(1,9)$ prior it is $0.116$, and
the release passes. Under the $\mathrm{Beta}(3,97)$ prior of the opening section it is $0.059$, and the release
passes with room to spare. The same 30 attacks and the same success give four bounds, and the
decision follows the prior. Ten more attacks without a success bring the exact bound to $0.113$ (one success in 40), and
the release passes on evidence alone.
\end{example}

The dashed line in Figure~\ref{c22:fig-bounds} shows the other cost a certificate carries. A release must pass
every declared coordinate, and the exact bounds for $M$ coordinates are each computed at level
$\alpha/M$, which by the union bound holds the chance that any of them misses at $\alpha$ without
assuming the coordinates are independent. That is the union bound of Chapter~\ref{ch:proposals}, and the engine's
\texttt{simultaneous\_upper\_bounds} computes it; its test enumerates the binomial law to confirm that
each bound misses with probability at most $\alpha/M$. The cost grows slowly. At a tolerance of $0.12$
the zero-success audit needs 24 attacks for one coordinate, 29 for two, 35 for four, 40 for eight
and 46 for sixteen. No prior enters the calculation, and none can shorten it.

The posterior has honest uses, and the engine keeps them. Before an audit is run, the question is
how many attacks to plan for, and a forecast answers it best when it keeps its own uncertainty. The
engine's posterior predictive gives the law of a future count as a weighted mixture of binomials
over the posterior, and its test confirms that the mixture is wider than the binomial obtained by
plugging in the posterior mean (the same fact as Proposition~\ref{c20:prop-mix}). A planner who sized the audit from the plug-in figure
would plan too few attacks and see the audit run long more often than expected. Both Bayesian
functions say in their documentation that they return planning figures, and the gate of
Chapter~\ref{ch:learning} refuses a planner's prediction offered in place of a bound.

Naledi took the figure to the chair with one question for the innovation group: what was their
prior made of? It had been fitted to the development attacks on the earlier six-vintage file, so it
held the weight of a few hundred attacks run on a different release. The chair wrote in the margin
that the group's posterior was a good reason to expect the audit to pass and no reason to skip it.

\section{Betting with a forecast}\label{c22:sec-betting}

The forecast earns its place inside the audit, where it can shorten the work without touching the
error. Chapter~\ref{ch:monitoring} built the sequential audit as a bet against the hypothesis that the release is
unsafe, in the manner of testing by betting \citep{shafer2021,waudbysmith2024}. Before attack $b$ the
auditor fixes a number $q_b$ below the tolerance, her wealth is multiplied after each attack by the
factor in Chapter~\ref{ch:monitoring}, and she certifies the first time the wealth reaches $1/\alpha$. The bet should be
close to the true risk: the expected audit length is shortest when $q_b$ equals the true risk, and
the true risk is what the auditor does not know. This is the rule of \citet{kelly1956}, who showed
that a gambler's wealth grows fastest when the bets match the true probabilities. A forecast is a
guess at it, and the engine turns a Bayesian forecast into bets.

The rule is simple. Start from a beta prior with parameters $a$ and $b_0$. Before attack $b$, compute
the posterior mean from the outcomes of attacks 1 to $b-1$ only,
\[\bar r_{b-1}=\frac{a+S_{b-1}}{a+b_0+b-1},\qquad S_{b-1}=\sum_{t<b}X_t,\]
and bet $q_b=\min\{\tau-\epsilon,\max(\epsilon,\bar r_{b-1})\}$ for a fixed $\epsilon\in(0,\tau/2)$. The engine's defaults are $a=1$,
$b_0=9$ and $\epsilon=\tau/10$, so at a tolerance of $0.12$ the first bet is the prior mean of $0.1$ and every bet
lies between $0.012$ and $0.108$. A planner's prediction can replace the default prior by choosing $a$
and $b_0$ so that the prior mean is the prediction. Proposition~\ref{c22:prop-bets} shows that the choice cannot hurt
the error bound, and that one change of timing can destroy it.

\begin{proposition}[Predictable Bayesian bets]\label{c22:prop-bets}
Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be attack outcomes in $\{0,1\}$ with $\mathcal F_b$ the information after attack $b$, let $\tau\in(0,1)$,
$\alpha\in(0,1)$ and $\epsilon\in(0,\tau/2)$, and let $E_n=\prod_{b\le n}(q_b/\tau)^{X_b}\{(1-q_b)/(1-\tau)\}^{1-X_b}$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item For any prior with $a,b_0>0$, the bet $q_b$ defined above is a function of $X_1,\dots,X_{b-1}$ and lies in $[\epsilon,\tau-\epsilon]$.
\item If $\Prob(X_b=1\mid\mathcal F_{b-1})\ge\tau$ for every $b$, then $(E_n)$ is a nonnegative supermartingale with $E_0=1$, and the audit that certifies when $E_n\ge1/\alpha$, stopped at any time and by any rule, certifies with probability at most $\alpha$.
\item If instead the bet may depend on $X_b$ itself, with $q_b=\tau-\epsilon$ when $X_b=1$ and $q_b=\epsilon$ when $X_b=0$, and the outcomes are independent with success probability exactly $\tau$, then for all small enough $\epsilon$ the audit certifies with probability one.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The second part holds for every prior, however wrong. A forecast of $0.01$ for a release whose true
risk is $0.11$ produces small bets, wealth that grows slowly or shrinks, and a long audit that may
end without a certificate; it cannot produce a false certificate more often than $\alpha$. The third
part is the reason the engine computes each bet from a shifted cumulative count and the test suite
checks that changing the outcome of attack $b$ leaves bet $b$ unchanged. The peeking bets of the
third part all lie inside $(0,\tau)$, so the range check on the bets cannot catch them. Only the
timing can.

The evidence run checked the bound where it is tightest. At a tolerance of $0.12$ and $\alpha=0.05$, it
simulated 1,000 audits of a release whose true risk is exactly $0.12$, each allowed up to 1,500
attacks with the default prior. The audit falsely certified in 46 of the 1,000, a rate of $0.046$
(the repository's run with seed 2026 gave the same count), and the exact 95 per cent upper bound on that rate is $0.058$. The point estimate is under the
promised $0.05$, as Proposition~\ref{c22:prop-bets} requires, and a thousand runs cannot place the rate more finely
than the interval shows.

Figure~\ref{c22:fig-audit} shows what the forecast buys on safer releases. At true risks of $0.03$ and $0.06$
every audit certified, after a median of 56 and 144 attacks (the repository's run reproduces both); at $0.09$ the median was 638
attacks, or 568 among the audits that certified, and 87.5 per cent of audits certified within the cap of 1,500 (the draft
printed 669, 585 and 85 per cent from its own 200 simulated audits). The comparison in the figure is the cheapest fixed design, the 24-attack audit that
certifies only if no attack succeeds. At a true risk of $0.03$ that design certifies with probability
$0.48$, at $0.06$ with probability $0.23$ and at $0.09$ with probability $0.10$. It is short because it gives
up at the first success, and a release with a real but acceptable risk produces successes. The
betting audit pays more attacks to tolerate them, and the forecast keeps that payment small when
the release is far from the line. Near the line nothing keeps it small. The rise from 144 attacks
at $0.06$ to 638 at $0.09$ is the behaviour the expected-length result of Chapter~\ref{ch:monitoring} predicts, and no
forecast changes it.

One feature of the run belongs in any report of it. The share certified and the median length at
each risk come from the same 200 simulated audits, and an audit that never certified counts its full
1,500 attacks in the median, so the median at $0.09$ is partly a statement about the cap. And the
guarantee rests on the condition in the second part of the proposition, that each attack's success
probability given the past is at least the tolerance when the release is unsafe. Fresh targets
drawn from the registered target law satisfy it. An audit that turns to easier targets once it has
seen a few successes does not, and Chapter~\ref{ch:monitoring} showed how that design fails.

Naledi planned the audit for Tom\'as's update in this form. She set the prior for the bets from the
innovation group's prediction, made on the six-vintage file, with the weight of twenty attacks so
that the audit's own outcomes would soon outweigh it, and fixed the cap, the tolerance and the
error share in the plan before registering it. If the group's forecast was right, the audit would
be short. If it was wrong in either direction, the audit would be longer, and the certificate
would mean the same.

\begin{example}[Naledi's bets]\label{c22:ex-bets}
A forecast of $0.03$ with the weight of twenty attacks is a $\mathrm{Beta}(0.6,19.4)$ prior. At a tolerance of $0.12$ the first bet is
$0.03$, and a failed attack multiplies the wealth by $0.97/0.88=1.102$, against $1.023$ under the default
bet of $0.1$. If no attack succeeds, the audit reaches a wealth of 20 at attack 28; the default prior
needs 37. Suppose the fourth attack succeeds. The bet then was $0.026$, so the wealth, $1.345$ after three
failures, is multiplied by $0.026/0.12$ and falls to $0.292$. The forecast moves to $0.067$ and the bets
with it, and the audit certifies at attack 52, against 55 for the default. With a second success at
attack 21 it certifies at 70, against 72. A confident forecast pays most on a clean run and costs
most at a success, and the two priors end close together once the audit's own outcomes outweigh them.
\end{example}

\section{Spending the budget before the outcome}\label{c22:sec-registry}

The last place a forecast can hurt a decision is after an audit fails. A model that predicted a
safe release, contradicted by an audit, is a strong temptation to try again, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:monitoring} measured what that costs. The extension's evidence run repeated the measurement: a
release with true risk $0.201$ against a tolerance of $0.2$, so that every certificate is false,
audited with 500 attacks per attempt and up to 20 attempts. When each attempt was judged at the
full 5 per cent, 50.2 per cent of 2,000 simulated programmes ended with a certificate (52.2 per
cent in the repository's run with seed 2026). When the 5 per cent was divided among the 20 attempts in advance, 4.0 per cent did
(3.95 per cent in the repository's run). These figures agree with the larger run of Chapter~\ref{ch:proposals}, and the remedy is the
same: every attempt pays from one budget. What the extension adds is the place where the payment is
made.

The ledger of Chapter~\ref{ch:proposals} debited the budget after an audit's outcomes were supplied, and that chapter
recorded the weakness: a caller could draw outcomes outside the ledger, discard a failure and never
pay for it. The engine's registry closes that gap on a single host. It is a SQLite database with
three tables: programmes with their total error budgets, plans frozen by their digests, and
attempts. To run an audit, the caller first reserves a share $\alpha_\ell$ against a registered plan.
The registry adds up what the programme has already spent, refuses the reservation if the total
would exceed the budget, and writes the attempt row, all inside one transaction that takes the
database's write lock before reading, so that two writers cannot both read the same balance and
both spend it, the lost update of \citet{berenson1995}. Only then does it return the attempt's audit
seed. An attempt is later closed as passed, failed or abandoned, and in each case its share stays
spent. The tests check both halves of the rule. A programme with a budget of $0.05$ that spends $0.03$
on a failed attempt and $0.02$ on a second is refused a third reservation of $0.001$, and four writers
racing to reserve $0.03$ each from a budget of $0.05$ produce exactly one reservation. (The draft says
the attempt row also carries a reservation time; the repository's table does not store one, which
is one reason the clock remains an open item below.)

\begin{proposition}[Spending before outcomes]\label{c22:prop-spend}
Let a programme with budget $\alpha_P$ run attempts $\ell=1,2,\dots$, and let $\mathcal G_{\ell-1}$ be everything recorded before
attempt $\ell$ draws its first outcome. Suppose the share $\alpha_\ell\ge0$ is fixed given $\mathcal G_{\ell-1}$, that $\sum_\ell\alpha_\ell\le\alpha_P$ on every
path, and that, whenever the release attempt $\ell$ audits is unsafe, the attempt certifies it with conditional probability at
most $\alpha_\ell$ given $\mathcal G_{\ell-1}$. Then the probability that any attempt certifies an unsafe release is at most
$\alpha_P$. The conclusion fails if attempts may draw outcomes before their share is recorded and leave
unrecorded the attempts that fail.
\end{proposition}

The proposition is the fixed-sample counterpart of the sequential result of Chapter~\ref{ch:monitoring}, and a plain
form of the alpha spending that clinical trials use to divide one error among interim looks
\citep{lan1983}. The registry is built to meet its conditions. Reservation before the first draw
makes each share depend only on what was recorded earlier. The refusal at the budget makes the sum
hold on every path, including paths on which a server fails mid-audit and the attempt is
abandoned. Each attempt receives its own audit seed, drawn at random when the budget is reserved
and recorded with the attempt, so that attempts draw fresh targets and audit draws stay separate
from the streams used in development. An earlier version derived the seed from the plan digest and
the attempt number, which anyone holding the plan could compute in advance; a development team that
could foresee the audit targets could tune to them. A recorded random seed can be replayed
afterwards and cannot be foreseen.

The registry has limits it states itself. Recomputing plan digests detects a plan edited after
registration; the engine's \texttt{verify\_hashes} lists any plan whose stored body no longer matches
its digest. The registry cannot say who wrote a row or when, because the clock and the file belong
to whoever runs it. An external timestamp, or a signed store run by someone outside the release
team, is still needed for that, and the claim ledger lists it as open.

Grace audited the registry three months after Tom\'as's update was released. She found two attempts
against the plan: the first abandoned when the attack server restarted part way through, the
second passed. Both had spent their shares, and the sum was inside the programme's budget. The
verification listed no altered plans. Her one finding was the clock: the reservation times came
from the same machine that ran the audits, and she recommended that the Bank anchor the registry's
daily state with the records office, which keeps its own time.

\section{The answer}

A forecast of attack success makes an audit cheaper in two places and nowhere else. It sizes the
plan, through a posterior predictive that keeps its own uncertainty, and it sets the bets of a
sequential audit, which is then short when the forecast is right and long when it is wrong, with
the same error either way. On the synthetic evidence the forecast bets certified safe releases at
risks of $0.03$ and $0.06$ every time, after a median of 56 and 144 attacks, while the false
certification rate at the tolerance stayed at $0.046$. What must stay out of the forecast's reach is
the certificate itself. A credible bound is smaller than the exact bound for the priors in common
use, and the difference is paid for in false certificates: about $0.15$ at the tolerance when a
zero-success audit is sized to the $\mathrm{Beta}(1,9)$ prior, and certainty under a prior narrow enough to
pass on its own. The planner's labels must be integer counts from executed attacks, its depth must
beat a logistic baseline on held-out groups before it is credited, and on the extension's data it
did so by about $0.0026$ nats per trial. A language model may only propose, through an intake that
refuses outcomes. And every attempt, failed or abandoned, pays its share of a budget before its
first outcome is drawn. A model that predicts well makes the audit cheaper. It cannot stand in for
the audit, because no model's accuracy appears anywhere in the bound the committee signs.

\begin{inpractice}[In practice: letting a forecast plan the audit]
Train planners only on integer counts from executed attacks, and refuse rates. Report a logistic
model fitted to the same counts beside any deeper model, on groups (borrowers, connected groups,
and later periods) kept out of training, and record any fit that did not converge. Calibrate with a
temperature on a separate partition and treat the result as valid only for data like it. Let a
language model propose through a strict intake, a named reviewer and an approved evaluator; never
accept a count, a risk or a label from it. Use a posterior to size an audit and to set its bets,
never as its bound. Fix every bet before its outcome, and register the plan, the cap, the tolerance
and the share before the first draw. Reserve each attempt's share before it starts, keep failed and
abandoned attempts in the ledger, and anchor the registry's clock outside the release team.

\texttt{binomial\_cross\_entropy}, \texttt{calibrate\_temperature}, \texttt{grouped\_split}, \texttt{recover\_count}). The intake is
\texttt{privacy\_risk.proposals} (\texttt{validate\_proposals}, \texttt{freeze\_plan}) and the evidence contract
\texttt{privacy\_risk.evidence} (\texttt{MeasurementRecord}, \texttt{training\_rows}). Bounds and planning figures are in
\texttt{privacy\_risk.uncertainty} (\texttt{binomial\_upper}, \texttt{simultaneous\_upper\_bounds}, \texttt{beta\_posterior\_upper},
\texttt{posterior\_predictive\_counts}); the audit is \texttt{privacy\_risk.betting} (\texttt{predictable\_bets}, \texttt{log\_wealth},
\texttt{run\_audit}); the ledger is \texttt{privacy\_risk.registry.Registry} (\texttt{register\_plan}, \texttt{reserve}, \texttt{close},
\texttt{verify\_hashes}). \texttt{scripts/chapter22\_evidence.py} regenerates the numbers of this chapter, and \texttt{tests/test\_ext\_planner.py} tests them. The draft also names
\texttt{drisk proposals} and \texttt{drisk registry} command-line entries; this repository has no such commands. Algorithm~22.1 makes
the bets predictable by counting only the outcomes before each attack, and Algorithm~22.2 runs the audit with them; their
checks replay Example~\ref{c22:ex-bets}.
\end{inpractice}

\begin{mdframed}[linecolor=gray!60,backgroundcolor=gray!5]
\textbf{Algorithm 22.1.} Predictable bets from a beta forecast.\par\smallskip
\textit{Input.} Attack outcomes $X_1,\dots,X_n$; a tolerance $\tau$; prior parameters $a$ and $b_0$ (by default 1 and 9); a margin $\epsilon$ (by default $\tau/10$).\par
\textit{Output.} The bets $q_1,\dots,q_n$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Refuse the inputs unless $0<\tau<1$ and $0<\epsilon<\tau/2$.
\item For each attack $b$, count the successes before it, $S_{b-1}=\sum_{t<b}X_t$, with $S_0=0$. The outcome $X_b$ is not used.
\item Form the posterior mean $\bar r_{b-1}=(a+S_{b-1})/(a+b_0+b-1)$.
\item Bet $q_b=\min\{\tau-\epsilon,\max(\epsilon,\bar r_{b-1})\}$, which lies in $[\epsilon,\tau-\epsilon]$ (Proposition~\ref{c22:prop-bets}, part 1).
\end{enumerate}
\textit{Checks, each run against the software by the test suite:}
\begin{itemize}
\item A forecast of $0.03$ with the weight of twenty attacks, so $a=0.6$ and $b_0=19.4$, $\tau=0.12$ and a success at attack 4 (Example~\ref{c22:ex-bets}), bet at attack 4: 0.0261.
\item The same, bet at attack 5: 0.0667.
\item The same with attack 4 failing instead, bet at attack 4, which its own outcome cannot change: 0.0261.
\item The default prior, first bet: 0.1.
\item The default prior after two successes in two attacks, third bet, held at $\tau-\epsilon$: 0.108.
\item A margin $\epsilon=\tau/2$: refused.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\textbf{Algorithm 22.2.} The betting audit.\par\smallskip
\textit{Input.} Attack outcomes $X_1,\dots,X_n$; a tolerance $\tau$; a level $\alpha$; the prior and margin of Algorithm 22.1.\par
\textit{Output.} Whether the audit certifies, the attack at which it stopped, and the number of attacks used.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Refuse the inputs unless $0<\alpha<1$. Compute the bets $q_b$ by Algorithm 22.1.
\item Refuse the inputs unless every outcome is 0 or 1, there is one bet per outcome, and every bet lies strictly between 0 and $\tau$.
\item After each attack $b$, compute the log wealth $\log E_b=\sum_{t\le b}\big[X_t\log(q_t/\tau)+(1-X_t)\log\{(1-q_t)/(1-\tau)\}\big]$.
\item Stop at the first $b$ with $E_b\ge1/\alpha$ and certify, having used $b$ attacks.
\item If the wealth never reaches $1/\alpha$, do not certify; all $n$ attacks are used.
\end{enumerate}
\textit{Checks:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Forecast prior of Example~\ref{c22:ex-bets}, $\tau=0.12$, $\alpha=0.05$, no success, attack at which the audit certifies: 28.
\item The default prior, no success: 37.
\item Forecast prior, a success at attack 4: 52.
\item Forecast prior, successes at attacks 4 and 21: 70.
\item Forecast prior, wealth after three failures and a success at attack 4: 0.2924.
\item The default prior with only 20 attacks, all failing, attacks used: 20.
\item The same, whether the audit certifies: false.
\item A level $\alpha=1$: refused.
\item An outcome recorded as 2: refused.
\end{itemize}
\end{mdframed}

\section{Proofs}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{c22:prop-count}]
For the first part, the penalty does not involve $h_i$, and the unpenalised loss contains $h_i$ only in
the term $[B_i\log(1+e^{h_i})-K_ih_i]/N$. Its derivative is $[B_ie^{h_i}/(1+e^{h_i})-K_i]/N=(B_ip_i-K_i)/N$.
Derivatives with respect to the parameters follow by the chain rule through each layer.

For the second part, let $\tilde x_i=(x_i,1)$ and $\beta=(w,c)$, so that $h_i=\tilde x_i^\top\beta$. The function
$g(h)=\log(1+e^h)$ has $g''(h)=p(1-p)>0$ with $p=1/(1+e^{-h})$, and $-K_ih_i$ is linear in $\beta$. The Hessian of
the loss in $\beta$ is therefore
\[\frac1N\sum_iB_ip_i(1-p_i)\tilde x_i\tilde x_i^\top+\lambda I,\]
a positive semidefinite matrix plus $\lambda I$ with $\lambda>0$, which is positive definite. The loss is therefore strictly
convex. It is continuous, and each data term is nonnegative: since $g(h)\ge\max(0,h)$ and $0\le K_i\le B_i$, we have
$B_ig(h_i)-K_ih_i\ge(B_i-K_i)h_i\ge0$ when $h_i\ge0$ and $B_ig(h_i)-K_ih_i\ge-K_ih_i\ge0$ when $h_i<0$. The loss is therefore
at least $\lambda\|\beta\|^2/2$ and tends to infinity as $\|\beta\|\to\infty$. A continuous coercive function attains its
minimum, and a strictly convex function has at most one minimiser.

For the third part, let $\hat r$ be $K/B$ rounded to $d$ places, so that $|\hat r-K/B|\le\frac12 10^{-d}$. Then
$|B\hat r-K|\le\frac12B10^{-d}<\frac12$ when $B<10^d$, and the nearest integer to $B\hat r$ is $K$. For the second
statement, suppose $d\ge1$ and $B\ge2\cdot10^d$, and round halves upwards. The rates that round to $5\cdot10^{-d}$ form the
half-open interval $[4.5\cdot10^{-d},5.5\cdot10^{-d})$, which lies inside $[0,1]$ and has length $10^{-d}$. A half-open
interval of length $L$ contains at least $\lfloor LB\rfloor$ points of the grid $\{k/B\}$, and here $LB\ge2$, so two counts
$k$ and $k+1$ publish the same rate, and no rule can recover both from it.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{c22:prop-temp}]
The map $z\mapsto1/(1+e^{-z/T})$ is strictly increasing for every $T>0$, which gives the first part. For the
second, write $s=1/T>0$ and $g(s)=N^{-1}\sum_i[B_i\log(1+e^{sz_i})-K_isz_i]$, which is the binomial cross-entropy of
$p(T)$ up to a constant free of $s$. Differentiating twice,
\[g''(s)=\frac1N\sum_iB_iz_i^2\,\sigma(sz_i)\{1-\sigma(sz_i)\},\qquad\sigma(u)=1/(1+e^{-u}),\]
which is strictly positive because some $z_i\ne0$ and every $B_i\ge1$. So $g$ is strictly convex on $(0,\infty)$ and has at
most one minimiser on any interval. The map $u=\log T\mapsto s=e^{-u}$ is strictly decreasing, and a strictly
convex function composed with a strictly monotone map is strictly quasi-convex: it decreases and then increases,
or is monotone. A bounded search for the minimum of a unimodal function in $\log T$, as the engine performs,
therefore seeks a single point.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{c22:prop-credible}]
For the first part, fix $K<B$. The exact bound is the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of $\mathrm{Beta}(K+1,\,B-K)$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:estimate}), with density $f_1$, and the
credible bound is the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of $\mathrm{Beta}(a+K,\,b+B-K)$, with density $f_2$. Their ratio is
\[\frac{f_2(x)}{f_1(x)}\propto x^{a-1}(1-x)^{b},\qquad0<x<1,\]
which is strictly decreasing when $a\le1$ and $b\ge1$, since the derivative of its logarithm is $(a-1)/x-b/(1-x)<0$. A density
ratio that decreases means the second law puts relatively more weight on small values: for every $x\in(0,1)$,
$F_2(x)-F_1(x)=\int_0^x(f_2-f_1)$ is positive. To see this, note that $f_2-f_1$ integrates to zero over $(0,1)$ and, because the
ratio decreases strictly and is not constant, $f_2>f_1$ below some point $x_0$ and $f_2<f_1$ above it; so the integral from 0 to $x$
increases on $(0,x_0)$, decreases on $(x_0,1)$, starts at zero and ends at zero, and is positive in between. Let $u_1$ be the
exact bound, so $F_1(u_1)=1-\alpha$. Then $F_2(u_1)>1-\alpha$, and since $F_2$ is continuous and strictly increasing, its $(1-\alpha)$
quantile lies strictly below $u_1$.

For the second part, take $p$ with $U_{a,b}(0)<p<U_{\rm CP}(0)$. If $K=0$ then $U_{a,b}(K)<p$, so the event $\{p\le U_{a,b}(K)\}$ lies inside
$\{K\ge1\}$, whose probability is $1-(1-p)^B$. The exact bound at $K=0$ is $1-\alpha^{1/B}$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:estimate}), so $p<1-\alpha^{1/B}$ gives
$(1-p)^B>\alpha$, and the coverage is less than $1-\alpha$.

For the third part, a release with true risk $\tau$ is certified at least whenever $K=0$, because $U_{a,b}(0)\le\tau$. That event has
probability $(1-\tau)^B$, and $\tau<U_{\rm CP}(0)=1-\alpha^{1/B}$ gives $(1-\tau)^B>\alpha$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{c22:prop-bets}]
For the first part, $S_{b-1}$ is a function of $X_1,\dots,X_{b-1}$, and so is $\bar r_{b-1}$, which is well defined because $a+b_0+b-1>0$.
Clipping maps it into $[\epsilon,\tau-\epsilon]$, an interval inside $(0,\tau)$ because $\epsilon<\tau/2$. No property of the prior beyond $a,b_0>0$ is used.

For the second part, write $p_b=\Prob(X_b=1\mid\mathcal F_{b-1})$. Since $q_b$ is fixed given $\mathcal F_{b-1}$,
\[\E\Big[\frac{E_b}{E_{b-1}}\,\Big|\,\mathcal F_{b-1}\Big]=p_b\frac{q_b}\tau+(1-p_b)\frac{1-q_b}{1-\tau}=1+\frac{(p_b-\tau)(q_b-\tau)}{\tau(1-\tau)}.\]
Under the hypothesis $p_b\ge\tau$, and $q_b<\tau$ by the first part, so the product in the numerator is at most zero and the
conditional expectation is at most one. Every factor is positive, so $E_b\ge0$, and $E_0=1$. Hence $(E_n)$ is a test
supermartingale, and Ville's inequality gives $\Prob\{\sup_nE_n\ge1/\alpha\}\le\alpha$. The audit certifies only on that event,
whatever time it stops and whatever rule chooses the time; a cap on the number of attacks removes paths from the event
and cannot enlarge it.

For the third part, each factor is $(\tau-\epsilon)/\tau$ when $X_b=1$ and $(1-\epsilon)/(1-\tau)$ when $X_b=0$, so $\log E_n$ is a sum of
independent, identically distributed terms with mean
\[\mu_\epsilon=\tau\log\frac{\tau-\epsilon}\tau+(1-\tau)\log\frac{1-\epsilon}{1-\tau}.\]
As $\epsilon\to0$, $\mu_\epsilon\to(1-\tau)\log\{1/(1-\tau)\}>0$, so $\mu_\epsilon>0$ for all small enough $\epsilon$. By the strong law of large numbers
$n^{-1}\log E_n\to\mu_\epsilon$ almost surely, so $\log E_n\to\infty$ and the wealth passes $1/\alpha$ with probability one, although the
release sits exactly at the tolerance.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{c22:prop-spend}]
Let $A_\ell$ be the event that attempt $\ell$ certifies an unsafe release, with $A_\ell$ empty for attempts that never run, which
carry $\alpha_\ell=0$ without loss. By the union bound and the tower property,
\[\Prob\Big(\bigcup_\ell A_\ell\Big)\le\sum_\ell\Prob(A_\ell)=\sum_\ell\E\big[\Prob(A_\ell\mid\mathcal G_{\ell-1})\big]\le\sum_\ell\E[\alpha_\ell]=\E\Big[\sum_\ell\alpha_\ell\Big]\le\alpha_P,\]
where the exchange of sum and expectation holds because every term is nonnegative. The argument needs each attempt's
share to be in the sum, whatever its outcome. If an attempt may draw its outcomes first and be recorded only when it
passes, the recorded shares can sum to at most $\alpha_P$ while the unrecorded failures go uncounted; the number of attempts
actually made is then unbounded, and by the repeated-attempts result of Chapter~\ref{ch:monitoring} the chance that one of them
certifies an unsafe release, each certifying with some fixed positive probability, tends to one.
\end{proof}
